# Intro to Chemistry Rubrics and Answer Key

This key supports the remote materials pack. Use it to check reasoning, compare worked examples, and identify common misconception patterns before revising a response.

## General CER Rubric

Strong:

1. Claim is specific, testable, and directly answers the prompt.
2. Evidence is accurate, cited from the provided source, and includes units when relevant.
3. Reasoning explains why the evidence supports the claim and addresses limits or uncertainty.
4. Model and vocabulary connect visible evidence to particles, formulas, equations, or graphs.

Proficient:

1. Claim answers the prompt but may be broad.
2. Evidence is mostly accurate and relevant.
3. Reasoning connects evidence to claim with a reasonable explanation.
4. Most target terms are used correctly.

Developing:

1. Claim is vague or partly off-topic.
2. Evidence is missing, weak, or uncited.
3. Reasoning restates evidence without explaining why it matters.
4. Terms are used loosely, or the response confuses observation with inference.

## Measurement Tables Key

Use the measurement tables to separate arithmetic from chemistry evidence:

1. Table A is easiest to interpret because units and precision are consistent.
2. Table B can be used after conversion.
3. Table C is not reliable by itself because the numbers lack units and measurement labels.
4. A strong response explains that arithmetic can be correct while the chemistry conclusion is wrong if the unit context is missing.

Worked conversion examples:

1. `0.0248 kg = 24.8 g`
2. `0.050 L = 50.0 mL`
3. `295 K ≈ 21.9 C`

Common revision target: a response that says "Table B is wrong" is too broad. Table B is repairable because the units are present; Table C is the one that lacks enough context.

Scientific notation and precision examples:

1. `0.000425 g` is `4.25 x 10^-4 g`.
2. `2.50 mL` has three significant figures, while `2.5 mL` has two.
3. If an expected mass is `25.0 g` and a measured mass is `24.5 g`, the percent error is `2.0%`.
4. A strong data-quality response names units, precision, and uncertainty rather than only reporting a calculator result.

## Model Comparison Key

A strong comparison chooses the model based on the question being answered:

1. For composition, `H₂O` is efficient because it shows the fixed hydrogen-to-oxygen ratio.
2. For particle spacing or motion, a particle diagram or simulation is better.
3. For a change over time, a graph is usually stronger.
4. The limitation should be specific: a balanced equation conserves atoms, but it does not show reaction speed, energy, or mechanism unless extra information is supplied.

Sample answer:

1. A heating curve is best for explaining why temperature can pause during boiling because the flat graph region directly shows temperature over time.
2. It is not enough by itself to show what particles are doing, so a particle diagram should be added for the reasoning part.

## Phenomena Case Library Key

Strong case explanations connect a visible observation to a model and name the evidence limit. Expected connections include:

1. Water cohesion and surface tension: visible beading or doming supports a model of attraction between water molecules, but it does not directly show individual molecules.
2. Lava-lamp density: oil and water separate because density and intermolecular interactions differ; gas bubbles can temporarily reduce the effective density of colored water droplets or carry liquid upward.
3. Oobleck: sudden-force resistance shows that simple solid/liquid categories are models with limits, not perfect boxes.
4. Apple, water, and balloon: solids keep shape, liquids take container shape while staying close together, and gases spread through available space.
5. Soda and balloon: opening or changing pressure lets dissolved carbon dioxide leave solution and enter the gas space.
6. Elephant toothpaste: foam is trapped gas from a decomposition reaction; the catalyst changes rate, not the amount of atoms available.
7. Baking soda volcano: gas production supports an acid-base reaction model because carbon dioxide is produced.
8. Invisible ink: the starting liquid can be discussed as a solution, while the revealed message requires separate reaction or oxidation evidence.
9. Mixture separation: the correct method depends on particle size, solubility, and whether the mixture is homogeneous or heterogeneous.

Common revision target: do not treat a dramatic demonstration as the explanation. The response should name the observation, chemistry idea, evidence, and model limit.

For the supplied states, S/L/G retain H₂O identity while arrangement and motion differ. Ice and water can coexist at the melting boundary. A textual particle comparison meets the same target as simulation screenshots.

The fictional drop-count means are water12 and oil6; predictions retain equal-drop and surface assumptions. Coin geometry changes do not determine a new count from these inputs alone. Oil's model density is lower than water's; adding a gas bubble can lower the combined density enough to rise, and losing gas reverses that model motion.

Oobleck's slow-flow/tap/release pattern supports a stress-dependent suspension model. It does not establish a new substance or a permanent solid phase. Carbonation releases previously dissolved CO₂; the volcano model instead forms CO₂ in a reaction. Their bubbles require different causal explanations.

Use the supplied text states when a video or simulation is unavailable, and keep any observed original-video features separately attributed.

## Reaction Projects Key

Strong reaction-project responses include:

1. Introduction to Chemical Reactions:
   - Phase changes are physical changes unless evidence shows new substances.
   - In `CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O`, the subscript in `CH₄` means four hydrogen atoms in one methane molecule.
   - The coefficient `2` before `O₂` means two oxygen molecules, and oxygen is a reactant in the combustion model.
   - In `4Fe(s) + 3O₂(g) → 2Fe₂O₃(s)`, `Fe` is iron, `(s)` means solid, `(g)` means gas, and `(l)` would mean liquid.
2. Elephant Toothpaste:
   - Yeast acts as a catalyst in the simplified activity.
   - Hydrogen peroxide decomposes into water and oxygen.
   - Oxygen gas gets trapped by soap as foam.
   - Scaling the activity changes amount without changing the underlying reaction model.
3. Making a Volcano:
   - Vinegar and baking soda are modeled as an acid-base reaction that produces carbon dioxide gas.
   - The bubbles or foam are reaction evidence because the model produces a gas.
   - The classroom volcano is an analogy and does not use the same process as real molten-rock volcanism.

Common revision target: do not use dramatic appearance as the proof. The response should identify reactants, products or likely products, reaction evidence, and the limitation of the model or demonstration.

In supplied models S/L, catalase in yeast is the modeled catalyst. Three times the reacting peroxide count yields three times the O₂ count under complete conversion, while foam height is not a calibrated gas measurement. Other video catalysts require their own source observations. The volcano equation preserves C,H,O and Na; actual rust and volcanoes have more detailed structures and mechanisms than these introductory models.

## Solutions Projects Key

Strong solutions-project responses include:

1. Making Invisible Ink:
   - Lemon juice and water form a mixture that can be discussed as a solution.
   - Dilution can make the dried mark harder to see.
   - The later darkening is separate reaction evidence, commonly explained with oxidation or heat-triggered chemical change in an introductory model.
2. Solution Simulation:
   - The solute is the substance being dissolved and the solvent does the dissolving.
   - In water, polar orientation and charge attractions help explain why ions spread through solution.
   - `NaNO₃` contains sodium, nitrogen, and oxygen.
   - `NaCl` contains sodium and chlorine.
   - `CuSO₄` contains copper, sulfur, and oxygen.
   - `KBr` contains potassium and bromine.
3. Separating Mixtures:
   - Rocks, gravel, and sand are heterogeneous and can be separated by particle size.
   - Sand and salt can be separated by dissolving the salt and filtering sand in a model.
   - Saltwater is homogeneous at the visible scale and requires removing solvent, such as by evaporation in a diagram or time-lapse model, to recover salt.

Common revision target: do not say a dissolved substance disappears. It is distributed at a particle scale that is not visible directly.

Drying the ink model removes water and leaves residue. The later stipulated reaction is a separate change; color alone cannot identify its chemical mechanism. Citric acid is one component, not a complete description of lemon juice.

For KBr, water oxygen faces K⁺ and water hydrogens face Br⁻. NaNO₃ and CuSO₄ retain their polyatomic ions in the supplied model. An unspecified ink dye cannot be assigned an ionic formula from color. Filtration retains sand but permits dissolved ions through; an evaporation model removes solvent rather than making dissolved salt vanish.

4. Trail mix and granite are heterogeneous mixtures.
5. Light is not matter in this context.

## Isotope Key

Carbon-12, carbon-13, and carbon-14 are all carbon because they have six protons. They differ by neutron count and mass number. The simplified chlorine average is 35.5 because `(35 x 0.75) + (37 x 0.25) = 35.5`.

The claim set is false, true, false. Adding an electron changes charge, not isotope; carbon-14's stated neutral counts are correct; chlorine's average is not an individual atom's mass number.

For the six notation cases, (protons,neutrons,electrons) are C-12=(6,6,6), C-14=(6,8,6), Na-23⁺=(11,12,10), Cl-35⁻=(17,18,18), O-16²⁻=(8,8,10), Mg-24²⁺=(12,12,10).

The supplied daughter (atomic number,mass number) pairs are alpha=(82,208), beta-minus=(7,14), beta-plus=(8,18), gamma=(43,99): lead-208,nitrogen-14,oxygen-18 and technetium-99 respectively. Gamma changes nuclear energy without changing these counts. After three half-lives, 800/8=100 parent nuclei are expected, or 12.5%; an individual decay time is not predicted.

| ---- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A    | 8 protons identifies oxygen; mass number is 16; neutral electron count is 8.                                                          |
| B    | Carbon has 6 protons; carbon-14 has 8 neutrons; neutral carbon has 6 electrons.                                                       |

| D    | 17 protons identifies chlorine; mass number is 35; 18 electrons gives `-1` charge.                                                    |

Common revision target: if the element name changes when only electrons change, the response is confusing ion charge with element identity.

For particle-count cases P1–P5, the answers are carbon-12 neutral, sodium-23 charge +1, chlorine-35 charge -1, oxygen-16 charge -2 and magnesium-24 charge +2. Show mass number=p+n and charge=p−e separately.

## Ion Formula Key

Examples: `NaCl`, `KF`, `MgO`, `CaBr₂`, `Al₂O₃`, `NH₄NO₃`. A correct explanation balances total positive and negative charge without changing the ion identities.

Worked charge-balance examples:

1. `Mg²⁺` and `Cl⁻`: one magnesium ion contributes +2, so two chloride ions are needed for -2 total. Formula: `MgCl₂`.
2. `Al³⁺` and `O²⁻`: two aluminum ions contribute +6 and three oxide ions contribute -6. Formula: `Al₂O₃`.
3. `NH₄⁺` and `NO₃⁻`: charges balance one-to-one. Formula: `NH₄NO₃`.

Common revision target: do not change chloride into `Cl²⁻` to make the charges work. Change the number of ions, not the ion charge.

3. `CaF₂`: calcium fluoride
4. `NH₄NO₃`: ammonium nitrate

Covalent examples should include:

## Periodic Trend Key

Trend checks:

1. Across period 2 from Li to F, atomic radius generally decreases while attraction for electrons generally increases.
2. Na is larger than Li because it is lower in group 1.
3. Cl is larger than F but still strongly attracts electrons because both are halogens.

A strong reference comparison names what each source contributed:

2. RSC is useful for interactive trends, element pages, histories, and richer context.
3. IUPAC is useful for standard names, symbols, and official periodic-table framing.
4. Ptable is useful for quick interactive scanning and filtering.

For symbol-word work:

2. `Co` is cobalt and is not the same as writing separate carbon and oxygen symbols unless the intended symbols are `C` and `O`.
3. A complete answer lists full element names, atomic numbers, and at least one property note or source note.

Common revision target: do not cite "the periodic table" as a source without naming which table or which fact was checked.

## Bonding and Formula Key

Core checks:

1. Metal plus nonmetal formulas are usually introduced as ionic at this level.
2. Nonmetal plus nonmetal formulas are usually introduced as covalent.
3. `NaCl` and `MgCl₂` should be described as formula units, not separate molecules.
4. Water and carbon dioxide both have three atoms, but water is bent while carbon dioxide is linear.

The extended twelve-card sort adds ionic Al₂O₃ and KBr. C and SiO₂ need structural context before being called separate covalent molecules. Carbon allotropes and common silica networks show why composition and molecular structure are different claims.

## Lewis Structure Key

Strong Lewis-style work shows a defensible electron count and a structure clue, not just a memorized drawing.

| Formula | Expected First-Pass Reasoning                                                                               |
| ------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `H₂`    | One shared pair gives each hydrogen two electrons.                                                          |
| `O₂`    | Twelve valence electrons support a double bond and two lone pairs on each oxygen in the common intro model. |
| `H₂O`   | Oxygen forms two O-H bonds and has two lone pairs, so the shape is bent rather than linear.                 |
| `CO₂`   | Carbon is central with two double bonds to oxygen, producing a linear model.                                |
| `CH₄`   | Carbon forms four single bonds to hydrogen and has no lone pairs.                                           |
| `NH₃`   | Nitrogen forms three N-H bonds and has one lone pair, giving a pyramidal shape in the intro model.          |

Common revision target: a formula gives atom count but not shape. The answer must reference bonding and lone pairs when explaining why `H₂O` and `CO₂` differ.

## Heating Curve Key

## Phase Diagram Key

Strong phase-diagram reasoning treats the diagram as a map of conditions:

1. Point A is solid, B is liquid, and C is gas in the simplified model.
2. Point D is on the melting/freezing boundary.
3. Point E is on the boiling/condensing boundary. Point F is approximately on the ice/vapor boundary at -10 C and 0.0026 atm; this pressure is about 0.26 kPa.
4. Moving right usually increases temperature; moving up increases pressure.
5. A response should state both coordinates before naming the state.

Sample answer:

1. At 25 C and 3 atm, the model favors liquid rather than gas because pressure is higher than ordinary 1 atm conditions.
2. B and G both favor liquid water at 25 C, at 1 and 3 atm respectively. Increasing pressure along this comparison does not cross a phase boundary.
3. The table describes water. Carbon dioxide has a different phase diagram and no stable liquid at 1 atm. Selected points do not establish all boundaries between them.

## Energy Phase and Gas Checkpoint Key

Strong responses connect each evidence source to a particle-level explanation:

1. Heating-curve flat regions involve energy transfer during phase change.
2. Phase-diagram answers must state both temperature and pressure.
3. Intermolecular-property answers should cite attraction evidence rather than familiarity.
4. Gas-scenario answers should explain pressure through collision frequency, collision strength, amount of particles, or available volume.

## Gas Law Scenario Key

Scenario checks:

1. Volume decrease at constant amount and temperature raises pressure because wall collisions become more frequent.
2. Temperature increase in fixed volume raises pressure because particles move faster and collide more strongly.
3. Adding particles in fixed volume raises pressure because more particles collide with the walls.
4. Increasing volume lowers pressure when amount and temperature are roughly constant.
5. Cooling lowers pressure in fixed volume because particle motion decreases.

Common revision target: do not explain gas behavior by saying particles get bigger or smaller. The introductory model should use motion, spacing, collisions, amount, and container volume.

## Intermolecular Property Key

Property checks:

1. At 1 atm, water boils near 100 C while solid carbon dioxide sublimes near -78.5 C. Carbon dioxide has no normal boiling point. Water and ethanol can instead be compared by normal boiling point: about 100 C and 78 C respectively.
2. Ethanol is mixed: it has a polar region and a nonpolar carbon chain.
3. Hexane-like oil is mostly nonpolar and does not mix well with water.

## Reaction Type Key

Reaction-type checks:

1. `2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O` is synthesis.
2. `2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂` is decomposition.
3. `CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O` is combustion.
4. `AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃` is double replacement and precipitation.

## Reaction Energy and Rate Key

Energy evidence and rate evidence are different claims:

1. A glow stick emitting light supports energy transfer.
2. Fading over time supports a rate or reactant-use observation.
3. Crushing a tablet supports a rate claim through surface area but does not by itself prove heat release.
4. A catalyst case supports faster reaction if the setup compares with and without the catalyst.
5. A cold-pack case supports energy absorption if temperature decreases in the surroundings.

Common revision target: do not write "the reaction is fast because it releases energy." Rate needs time-based evidence; energy needs heat, light, electrical, or other transfer evidence.

## Equilibrium Key

Strong equilibrium answers name opposing processes:

1. In a sealed carbonated drink, carbon dioxide leaves the liquid and dissolves back into the liquid.
2. At equilibrium those processes continue at matching rates.
3. Opening the container lowers pressure, so gas leaving becomes favored until a new condition is reached.
4. In saturated solution, dissolving and recrystallizing can both continue even when the visible amount of solid appears stable.

Common revision target: avoid defining equilibrium as "nothing is happening." The correct intro idea is dynamic balance.

## Redox Key

Strong redox answers name electron-transfer direction or electron-density change:

1. In rusting, iron is oxidized into iron-containing compounds while oxygen is reduced in the simplified model.
2. In a zinc/copper cell, zinc metal becomes zinc ions while copper ions become copper metal.
3. The visible evidence alone shows chemical change; redox language explains a more specific particle-level pattern.

Common revision target: redox is not a synonym for any reaction. It is appropriate only when electron transfer or changed electron ownership is part of the model.

## Concentration and pH Key

Core checks:

1. More solute at the same volume increases concentration.
2. Adding water to the same solute decreases concentration.
3. Doubling both solute and volume leaves concentration about the same.
4. Lower pH means more acidic.
5. Dilution usually moves pH toward neutral but does not mean the acid or base has disappeared.

Worked reasoning examples:

1. If solute doubles and volume is unchanged, concentration increases.
2. If solute is unchanged and water is added, concentration decreases because the same amount is spread through more solution.
3. If solute and volume both double, the ratio can stay about the same.
4. A pH change of 1 is not a small linear step; pH is logarithmic, so explanations should avoid treating pH 3 and pH 4 as one ordinary unit apart.

Relative to baseline2g/100mL, dissolved concentration is doubled in A, halved in B, unchanged in C, multiplied by2.5 in D, halved in E and doubled in F. D alone retains3g solid after equilibrium. F dissolves all8g because its doubled solution volume permits10g at the stipulated temperature. More total solute and more dissolved solute are distinct when solid remains.

## Molar Mass and Stoichiometry Key

Core checks:

1. `H₂O` is about `18 g/mol`.
2. `CO₂` is about `44 g/mol`.
3. `NaCl` is about `58.5 g/mol`.
4. `CaCO₃` is about `100 g/mol`.
5. `36 g` of water is about `2 mol`.
6. `2.5 mol` of carbon dioxide is about `110 g`.
7. In `2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O`, `1 mol` oxygen produces `2 mol` water.
8. In `CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O`, `1 mol` methane produces `2 mol` water.

Worked setup examples:

1. `H₂O`: `2(1) + 16 = 18 g/mol`.
2. `CO₂`: `12 + 2(16) = 44 g/mol`.
3. `NaCl`: `23 + 35.5 = 58.5 g/mol`.
4. `CaCO₃`: `40 + 12 + 3(16) = 100 g/mol`.
5. `36 g H₂O × (1 mol / 18 g) = 2 mol H₂O`.
6. `2.5 mol CO₂ × (44 g / 1 mol) = 110 g CO₂`.

Common revision target: coefficients from balanced equations compare moles, not grams directly. A grams-to-grams problem must go grams → moles → mole ratio → moles → grams.

Practice item9 gives6mol H₂O from3mol O₂ with enough hydrogen. For item10, the methane-to-water coefficient ratio is2mol H₂O/1mol CH₄.

Molarity states A–D are1.0,0.25,0.50,0.50mol/L. Divide by final solution volume. O₂ has32g/mol with the supplied practice values, so64g O₂ is2mol and18g H₂O is1mol.

## Water Formation Stoichiometry and Heat Key

Balanced equation: `2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O`.

Given `1 mol H₂` and `2 mol O₂`, the balanced ratio needs `2 mol H₂ : 1 mol O₂`. For `1 mol H₂`, only `0.5 mol O₂` is needed, so hydrogen is the limiting reactant.

Reacted:

1. `H₂` used: `1 mol`
2. `O₂` used: `0.5 mol`
3. `H₂O` produced: `1 mol`

Left over:

1. `H₂` left: `0 mol`
2. `O₂` left: `1.5 mol`

Heat result:

1. The reaction is exothermic.
2. If liquid water forms, `1 mol H₂O(l)` releases about `285.8 kJ`; with sign convention, `ΔH ≈ -285.8 kJ` for the mole produced in this case.
3. If water vapor forms, `1 mol H₂O(g)` releases about `241.8 kJ`.
4. A concise answer is: `1.5 mol O₂` left, `1 mol H₂O` produced, and about `286 kJ` released if liquid water forms.

Common revision target: do not consume all available oxygen just because it is listed. The limiting reactant controls product amount, and the heat value depends on product state.

## Quantitative Chemistry Checkpoint Key

Use these worked answers to check both the numerical result and the reasoning path:

1. `CaCO₃` is about `100 g/mol`: `40 + 12 + 3(16)`.
2. `58.5 g NaCl` is `1.0 mol NaCl` using `58.5 g/mol`.
3. In `2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O`, `4 mol H₂` produces `4 mol H₂O` because the ratio is `2:2`, or `1:1` after simplification.
4. For `1 mol H₂` and `2 mol O₂`, hydrogen limits the reaction, `1.5 mol O₂` remains, `1 mol H₂O` forms, and about `285.8 kJ` is released if liquid water forms.
5. For methane to water, the setup should go `g CH₄ → mol CH₄ → mol H₂O → g H₂O`; the `1:2` mole ratio comes from the balanced equation.

Common revision target: coefficients cannot be applied directly to grams because balanced equations compare particles or moles, not mass.

## Stoichiometry Error Key

Stoichiometry error checks:

1. Case A changes a formula instead of using coefficients.
2. Case B uses the wrong mole ratio.
3. Case C skips molar mass before using coefficients.
4. Case D gives the wrong final unit.
